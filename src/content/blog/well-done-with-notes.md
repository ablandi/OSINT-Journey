Today I sat on a call with senior analysts from the Skull Games Task Force and walked them through my findings on the practice case I've been working.

Their verdict: overall, well done.

I'll be honest — that landed. There's a difference between wondering if your work holds up and hearing people who do this for real say it does. But what I valued most wasn't the praise. It was the notes. Because the notes are where the craft is.

Here is what I took from that review:

**Officer safety comes first.** If something in a subject's background bears on the safety of the people who may have to make contact, it doesn't belong buried in the middle of a report. It belongs up front, flagged clearly. The analyst's job isn't just to find things — it's to hand law enforcement a picture they can act on safely.

**Use the most authoritative photo you can.** A report is only as credible as its weakest element. The best available official photograph does more for identification confidence than a handful of social media screenshots.

**Map the entities, not just the people.** People hide behind structures — businesses, registrations, shared addresses. Laying out the entities connected to every party, and the addresses they share, turns a pile of facts into a picture of how things actually connect.

**Show your path of discovery.** Every artifact in a report should carry its provenance: where it came from, how it was found, how another analyst could verify it. A finding nobody can retrace is just a claim.

None of those notes were about finding *more*. They were about presenting what I found the way the mission deserves — because on the other end of a real report is a real person waiting to be found, and real officers going to do the finding.

So: well done, with notes. I'll take that every time. The fall never keeps me, and the notes never stop coming — that's how this gets sharper.

Back to work.

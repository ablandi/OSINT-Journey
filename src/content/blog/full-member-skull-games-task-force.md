On October 2, I wrote that I was [officially onboarding](/blog/skull-games-its-official) as a volunteer OSINT analyst with the Skull Games Task Force — and I drew a line in that post: I was in the door, but I wasn't operational yet, and I wouldn't pretend otherwise.

Today I can say the other half of that sentence.

I've completed the requirements, and I'm now a full member of the Task Force.

The road between those two posts was not a formality. It was a training package that had to be worked, not skimmed. A practice case that took weeks of digging, reframing, and digging again. A review call with senior analysts who told me my work was [well done — and then gave me notes](/blog/well-done-with-notes) that showed me exactly where it wasn't sharp enough yet. And a fusion cell, where the work stops being a solo exercise and becomes what this actually is: a team effort, evaluated like one.

I want to be careful here, the same way I was careful in October. Full member doesn't mean finished product. I still have revisions open on that practice-case report. I still have more to learn than I can list. What it means is that the people who set the standard looked at my work and said it meets the standard to be trusted with the real thing — and that the responsibility gets heavier from here, not lighter. That's the part I don't want to rush past. On the other end of this work are real victims and real law enforcement partners. Being trusted with that is not a credential to collect. It's a weight to carry well.

A word about how I got here, because it matters to me: I did this while working a full-time day job, in the margins — early mornings, the hours around shifts. A conversation this past weekend gave me language for that. Paul made tents. The tents weren't the mission; they were how he supported himself while he did the mission. My day job is my tentmaking in this season. The mission is already happening. Today it just became official in a new way.

Thank you to the analysts who reviewed my work and told me the truth, notes and all. Thank you to the team that built a standard worth meeting instead of a door anyone can wander through. And thank God, who has a habit of putting me exactly where I'm supposed to be, usually by a road I wouldn't have drawn myself.

In October I said it's time to walk through the door. I have. Now the real work starts.

Back to work.

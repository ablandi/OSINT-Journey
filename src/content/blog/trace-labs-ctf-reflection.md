On August 8 I volunteered my OSINT skills to [Trace Labs](https://www.tracelabs.org/) to help with real missing person cases. It was a blast.

It also almost didn't happen, because my laptop decided to crash the day before.

## The day before

There is a particular kind of panic that comes from watching your machine die less than twenty-four hours before the thing you have been looking forward to for weeks. No boot. No recovery. Just a laptop that had picked the worst possible moment to quit on me.

I had spent all that time preparing — reading up on the rules, thinking through my workflow, getting my head right — and the one piece of hardware the whole thing depended on was gone. I will admit I took it personally.

But this work has a way of putting your inconveniences in perspective. Somewhere out there is a family that has not heard from someone they love in months or years. A dead laptop is not a tragedy. It is a problem to solve. So I solved it, and I showed up.

## What a Trace Labs CTF actually is

If you have not done one, a Trace Labs Search Party CTF is not a typical capture the flag. There are no artificial puzzles and no invented scenarios. The cases are real, active missing person cases, brought in with the cooperation of law enforcement. Everything you submit is open source intelligence — publicly available information only, gathered passively, with no contact with the subject, their family, or anyone connected to the case.

You submit findings as flags, and judges score them based on how useful the information could actually be to an investigator. Higher value goes to the things that move a case forward. That scoring model changes how you work. You are not hunting for anything that scores; you are trying to find the thing that helps.

## What it felt like

The clock does something to you. Hours of focused searching, one lead branching into three, three collapsing back into none. Pivoting off a username, a photo, a date, a place. Recognizing the moment a thread has gone cold and having the discipline to drop it and move on — the same [time management and pivoting](/blog/capella-graduation-milestone) skills I had been drilling in every course and practice scenario I have taken.

That was the part that surprised me most. Everything I had been building toward — [the ATII training](/blog/atii-follow-osint-data-review), [Uncharted's OSINT Fundamentals](/blog/osint-fundamentals-uncharted-review), [the KaseScenarios challenges](/blog/kasescenarios-sinister-obsession-review), [the PORP exam](/blog/porp-exam-review) — all of it was suddenly not practice. There was a real name attached to what I was doing, and a real family somewhere hoping someone finds something.

It is a strange mix of exhilarating and heavy. You are enjoying the work, genuinely, and at the same time you are aware of exactly why the work exists.

## What I took away from it

**Preparation is not the same as readiness.** I knew the techniques. What I did not have yet was the rhythm of applying them under a clock with real stakes.

**Verification matters more than volume.** It is tempting to submit everything you find. The submissions that mattered were the ones I could actually stand behind.

**Documentation is the job.** A finding no one else can follow or reproduce is not much of a finding.

**Show up anyway.** The crashed laptop was going to be a perfectly good excuse. I am glad I did not take it.

I have written before about [asking myself whether I was ready](/blog/am-i-ready-guardian-group). I do not think you ever fully answer that question. You just keep showing up, and let the work tell you where you still have growing to do.

I will be at the next one.

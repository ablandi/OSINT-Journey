On September 17, I finished the last three sessions of **Protect Your Family**, the counter-trafficking course from the [Skull Games Task Force](https://skullgames.io/) — two days ahead of the schedule I'd set for myself. I wrote up [sessions 1–5](/blog/protect-your-family-skull-games-course) earlier. This is the rest of the course.

## Session 6 — The Psychology of Exploitation

This session answers the question sitting underneath everything else in the course: *why does any of this work on a human mind?*

The frame is Maslow's hierarchy — physiological needs, safety, love and belonging, esteem, self-actualization — set alongside the Power & Control wheel, the catalog of domination tactics (intimidation, isolation, emotional abuse, coercion, and the rest) that show up wherever one person is being systematically controlled by another.

Who's vulnerable? Anyone with an unmet need. Runaways and foster youth are the classic targets, but the course is blunt that the "shame game" — secrets and blackmail — can trap kids from stable homes too. Vulnerability is situational, not just demographic.

The hook works by meeting needs: attention, affection, money, belonging, a dream. Love-bombing first, control later. The trafficker studies the victim the way an analyst studies a target — find the gap, fill it, own it.

And the reason victims don't just leave: fear — of violence, of the trafficker, of police — plus trauma bonding, shame, threats against family, no money or identification, and the belief that there's nowhere to go. Leaving feels more dangerous than staying. That's the design.

Social media poured gasoline on all of it. A trafficker can assess dozens of potential victims at once, harvest personal details straight from posts, and slide into private channels. Kids broadcast the exact vulnerabilities — loneliness, conflict at home, hunger for attention — that traffickers search for.

**My takeaway:** exploiters don't lead with force. They lead by finding the unmet need at one of those five levels and filling it on purpose. The need is real. The person filling it is the trap. Once you see that clearly, "why didn't they just leave" stops being a question you can ask with a straight face.

## Session 7 — The Telltale Signs

This is the practical session: the observable indicators of trafficking to watch for in the real world.

What to look for: tattoos or branding; clothing out of context; restricted movement, or someone who is never alone; a minor at a hotel during school hours or late at night; weight loss; bruises; depression; withdrawal and isolation; dropping grades; coded language on social media.

The session also teaches the vocabulary, which is worth memorizing — it shows up in OSINT work too. A **choose-up fee** is what a victim pays for a pimp's "protection." An **exit fee** is what she pays to avoid retaliation when she wants out. **Wifey** or **wife-in-law** means victims in a trafficker's stable. **Daddy** or **King** is the trafficker himself. A **2 Girl Special** is an offer of two girls, one usually a minor. And **GFE** — girlfriend experience — is the upsell: traffickers charge more for emotional-intimacy services.

Online, the red flags mirror session 6's psychology: oversharing personal struggles, posting location or school information, engaging strangers' direct messages, suggestive content, signaling loneliness or rebellion — anything advertising an unmet need or an opening.

And if you suspect something: ask privately, stay calm, never be accusatory, and never confront the suspected trafficker. *"Are you safe right now?" "Can you come and go as you please?" "Has anyone threatened you or your family?" "Are you being forced to do anything you don't want to do?" "Do you have your identification and phone?" "Is there someone I can call for you?"* Then report the situation rather than trying to extract the person yourself.

**My takeaway:** indicators are only useful if you know what they're for, and what they're for is recognition, not intervention. My lane is the same one I set back in session 2: observe carefully, and leave determination and action to law enforcement. You can't report what you can't see, and you can't protect your family from what you don't recognize.

## Session 8 — There is hope

The course closes the way it has to: with hope, and with concrete steps for what to do with everything you've just learned.

The core truths, compressed: trafficking is local and hidden in plain sight. It runs on grooming and psychology, not just force. Buyers drive it. Language like "the game" and "the life" hides the crime. And there is a real path to identify, interrupt, and restore.

**My takeaway:** hope here isn't a feeling, it's an assignment. I'll bring the indicator knowledge into Task Force work, keep tightening my own family's online safety posture, and teach what I've learned — the course's own closing point, and one I believe: the best way to learn is to teach.

## The course, complete

Eight sessions, finished September 17 — two days early. I posted the completion to LinkedIn the same day.

What the course did, start to finish, is what I said I wanted after session 5: it replaced vague conviction with specific, usable understanding. The definitions. The cycle. The making of a trafficker. The demand side. The psychology. The signs. And finally, what to do about all of it.

I came in wanting to help. I came out with a clearer picture of what helping actually requires.

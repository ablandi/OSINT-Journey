In 2019, before the pandemic, I had a crazy, noble idea.

"Influencer" was just becoming a legitimate thing, and I decided I was going to be an anti-trafficking influencer — someone who shined a light on human trafficking and spotlighted the organizations actually doing the work.

So I made videos. I posted them on Instagram, talking about the myths and facts of trafficking. Any chance I got — between taking out orders at Walmart, during downtime — I talked about it.

Let me be honest about what that took, because it sounds braver than it felt.

I'm an introvert. Putting my face on camera and talking about the darkest thing in the world was way outside my comfort zone. Every video was a negotiation with myself.

And I was a broke kid who had just moved out of his mom's house. The organizations I admired all had donate buttons, and I couldn't press them. Monthly donations weren't an option. What I had was time, and time was the only currency I could spend.

Here's what I didn't understand then: the videos were training me, even though I didn't have a name for it yet.

**Myth vs. fact is an OSINT discipline.** Every video forced me to check something before I said it. Is this actually true, or is it just something people repeat? What's the source? What's the difference between what feels true and what can be shown? That's the same muscle I use now on every case — verify before you speak, because getting it wrong costs more than getting it late.

**Listening beats performing.** I'm an introvert, and at first I thought that disqualified me from advocacy. It turns out the work doesn't need another loud voice. It needs people who pay attention — who notice the pattern, the pause, the account that doesn't add up. The camera taught me to speak. The quiet taught me to look.

**Time is a real donation.** I used to feel guilty that I couldn't give money. I've stopped apologizing for that. Time given consistently, over years, compounds into skill. The kid with a phone and no budget became the analyst with a degree, certifications, and a practice case under his belt. Same person. Same fight. Just more reps.

The path since then hasn't been linear — not even close. There were years where the fight felt far away and the day job felt like the whole story. But yesterday I had a great call with Jeremy Benjamin, and I don't know what the future holds.

What I do know is this: I'll still be here, using what I know, the information I have, and the time I have, to bring awareness and use OSINT to fight this beast — human trafficking.

I'm still that kid trying to find his way in this fight.

Don't count me out yet.

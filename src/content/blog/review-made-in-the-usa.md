*Made in the USA* is a different kind of book than the first two on this list — it’s not a researcher or advocate writing from the outside, it’s Aliesha Jordheim writing from the inside. This is a survivor’s account, and it reads like one: raw, personal, and unwilling to be neatly summarized into bullet points.

Reading a first-person survivor narrative changes the way you process everything else you’ve learned. The statistics and frameworks from books like *In Our Backyard* are important, but Jordheim’s story is a reminder that behind every one of those numbers is a specific person with a specific childhood, specific vulnerabilities that were exploited, and a specific, difficult road toward getting out and healing.

What struck me most was how the grooming process was described — gradual, deliberately confusing, and designed to make the victim doubt her own perception of what was happening to her. It’s a pattern that shows up again and again across survivor accounts, and seeing it laid out in Jordheim’s own words made it click for me in a way that secondhand summaries never quite did.

This book reinforced something I try to carry into every piece of awareness work I do now: survivors are not case studies, they are the most credible experts on what trafficking actually looks and feels like, and their voices should lead this conversation, not just supplement it.

**Takeaway:** A powerful, difficult, necessary read — this is the book I’d recommend if you want to understand trafficking from the inside rather than from a policy or statistics lens.

*Renting Lacey* was one of the first books I picked up after my eyes were opened to the reality of human trafficking, and it hit hard. Linda Smith, a former U.S. Congresswoman and founder of Shared Hope International, doesn’t write this as an abstract policy paper — she writes it as a call to see the girls behind the statistics.

The book centers on domestic minor sex trafficking here in the United States, which was the part that unsettled me most. It’s easy to picture trafficking as something that happens “over there,” in another country, to someone else’s children. Smith dismantles that assumption immediately. She walks through how vulnerable kids — runaways, foster youth, kids from broken homes — get identified, groomed, and sold, often within their own communities.

What stuck with me was how much of the book focuses on demand. Smith doesn’t let the conversation stay on traffickers alone; she pushes the reader to reckon with the fact that trafficking exists because there’s a market for it. That framing connected directly back to what I’d already learned from *Brain, Heart, World* — the demand side of this crisis is not separate from the supply side, it drives it.

As someone building a habit of OSINT-style research and awareness, this book gave me language and framework I still use: the idea that “Lacey” isn’t one girl, she’s a composite of thousands of real cases, and that specificity matters when you’re trying to actually help rather than just feel bad about a headline.

**Takeaway:** If you want a grounded, U.S.-focused introduction to domestic minor sex trafficking that avoids sensationalism while still being emotionally honest, this is a strong place to start.

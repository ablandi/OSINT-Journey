I'm currently working through **Protect Your Family**, a counter-trafficking course from the [Skull Games Task Force](https://skullgames.io/). I'm going through it alongside the participant workbook and keeping my own companion notes as I go.

I'm a child online safety advocate, and I want the investigative skills I've been building to serve the fight against trafficking. Sarah-Jane says it in the course better than I can: *"Do what you love to fight what you hate."*

I've finished sessions 1 through 5. Here's what each one covered and what I'm actually taking from it.

## Session 1 — Introduction

The course is introduced by Jeff Tiegs, the founder of Skull Games and a 26-year Army Special Forces veteran, and Sarah-Jane Vegas, a survivor and the founder of Stand 4 Justice.

The framing session sets the terms for everything that follows. Trafficking cuts across all demographics — there is no single victim profile. Grooming can start anywhere, from a teenager's social media account to a strip club. Victims are held in place by manipulation and fear, not usually by locks and chains. Buyers are the overlooked half of the problem. And the indicators are learnable, which is the entire reason a course like this exists.

**My takeaway:** words build up or tear down, and nowhere more than in a child's mind. Sarah-Jane internalized blame for her uncle's violence after being asked, *"Why did you open the door?"* One question, and a child carries the weight of an adult's crime.

The other thing that stayed with me is that her mother wasn't the villain in the story. God later used her mother to help Sarah escape the cycle of abuse. It's a reminder not to flatten people in these stories into roles.

## Session 2 — What is trafficking?

This session is about precision, and I appreciated that.

Sex trafficking means commercial sex induced through **force, fraud, or coercion** — or **any** commercial sex act involving a minor, regardless of force, fraud, or coercion. A pimp is a trafficker; the two are not different things. And the workbook reframes the word "prostitute" as what the person usually is: a trafficking victim.

It also breaks down pimp types:

- **Gorilla** — control through violence and intimidation.
- **Romeo** — control through manufactured love and affection.
- **CEO** — control through a business framing, with the victim sold on the idea that she's a partner in an enterprise.

**My lens:** I default to treating prostitution as potential sex trafficking, because trafficker involvement is the norm with rare exceptions. All pimps are traffickers, and buyers sustain the market that makes any of it profitable.

That's my working posture as an investigator, not a legal conclusion. Law enforcement and the courts determine charges. Keeping that line straight matters.

## Session 3 — The exploitation cycle

The workbook lays the cycle out in stages: **Assessment and Recruitment**, **Grooming**, **Breaking**, and **Maintenance**.

Recruitment happens anywhere trust can be built. A high school boyfriend. A classmate. A DM. There's no exotic mechanism required — just access and time.

The piece that hit hardest was the language. Traffickers call it **"The Game."** Victims and the people being groomed call it **"The Life."** Two names for the same thing, and both of them do work: one makes it sound like sport, the other makes it sound like an identity you belong to. The vocabulary is part of the trap.

**What it activated for me:** paying closer attention to how my family and I present ourselves online, learning the red-flag keywords so I can actually recognize them in the wild, and staying compassionate toward anyone caught in exploitation. Recognizing the pattern is only useful if you don't turn it into judgment of the person stuck inside it.

## Session 4 — Understanding the enemy

This session is about the trafficker: how one gets made, and why they're hard to prosecute.

The making of a pimp usually runs through trauma and generational cycles. The control is psychological warfare — mind games designed to keep victims compliant without needing constant physical force. And prosecutions are difficult because victims often won't talk, out of fear and trauma bonding. The person best positioned to testify is the person least able to.

Key terms from the workbook:

- **The Bottom** — the trafficker's main girl, who runs the stable and recruits.
- **The Stable** — the group of victims under one trafficker.
- **Branding** — marking a victim as property.

**My takeaway:** traffickers can be genuinely diabolical. One account in the material described a trafficker deliberately impregnating a victim and then using the child as collateral and a means of control. That's not impulse. That's strategy.

## Session 5 — Killing the fantasy

This session goes after the story buyers tell themselves.

The victims are not having a good time, no matter what they have to say. What gets said in that moment is survival, not consent.

Buyers are ordinary men across all demographics. There's no monster profile to look for, and that's exactly why demand is so hard to see and so easy to ignore. Strip clubs are a gateway into trafficking, not a safe zone. And policy matters — the legal environment shapes how much of this is possible and how it gets treated.

**What I'm already doing about it:** I've started researching South Carolina's trafficking and prostitution statutes, and I've set up a way to track new legislation as it moves. I want to be able to contact policymakers and raise awareness while bills are actually being decided, not after.

## Where I'm headed

Sessions 6 through 8 are now done — I finished the full course on September 17, two days ahead of schedule. The follow-up is here: [Finishing Skull Games' Protect Your Family Course: Sessions 6–8](/blog/protect-your-family-course-sessions-6-8).

What I'm getting is a clearer picture of what helping actually requires — the definitions, the patterns, the language, and the honesty to look at the demand side instead of only the supply.
